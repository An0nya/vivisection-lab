What this is

A working record of taking small open-weights models apart to see where their abilities physically live — and whether you can move them around by editing the layer stack at inference time, with no retraining. The method owes its origin to David Ng's RYS ("Repeat Your Self") layer-duplication work; the experiments here extend it onto a dense model, a mixture-of-experts, and a recurrent architecture it was never designed for, and pair it with a strict-grading discipline that keeps catching the pretty results lying.

Everything ran on a $599 Mac Mini. The original technique was for somebody with H100s. This is for somebody with a kitchen.

§The through-line

Run the same scalpel across enough specimens and the same handful of facts keep surfacing — strongly enough that they read less like quirks of one model and more like properties of how small transformers are built. Five of them recur on every page here.

Saturation. Every small base model arrives already maxed out on the easy stuff and floored on the hard stuff. If a battery has no headroom, surgery can't show a gain — so half the work is finding a probe that the baseline actually fails, but not by accident.

The depth U-curve. The two ends of the stack are load-bearing and fragile; the middle is a tolerant, redundant highway. Touch the input or output layers and the model dies; touch the middle and very little happens. Damage, like meaning, lives in the middle.

The rescue-prune. Some layers are worse than useless — the model reasons better with them removed. Pruning is a real intervention axis, not just the inverse of duplication.

The grader lies. A partial-credit math scorer rewards confident, well-formatted, wrong answers. On three separate models it manufactured "champions" that strict equality erased. Lead with the strict number, always.

Probes are hypotheses, not verdicts. A pretty geometric metric that agrees with your story is not evidence until you've shown it tracks the outcome you care about. The HRM page is the cautionary tale; it cost a full overnight.

On honesty Two of the most exciting findings in this notebook were wrong — a more-loops-helps result on HRM and a math leaderboard on Gemma — and both are kept on the page, with the autopsy. A lab notebook that only records the confirmed results isn't a lab notebook; it's a brochure. The negative results are the most reusable thing here.
A high-variance model, greedy single-shot scoring, and a corroborating-but-decoupled metric will together manufacture a clean, theory-shaped, completely false finding — and it will survive a casual robustness check. Sampling and verification are not polish. The methodology lesson · learned the hard way · §HRM

§Colophon & credits

Credit & method

The duplication technique is David Ng's RYS ("Repeat Your Self"), which he rode to the top of the open-LLM leaderboard; the no-chain-of-thought, tiny-output, partial-credit probe design is also his, and it elegantly sidesteps three confounds at once (the indecision tax, format brittleness, and sample-size starvation). The strict-equality regrade here doesn't refute that design — it extends it. Ng's own 2026 retrospective notes that he "never found a single duplication that generalised; could only boost one talent at another's expense," which independently matches the archetype split found on Gemma and Granite. Every architecture has its own neuroanatomy.
